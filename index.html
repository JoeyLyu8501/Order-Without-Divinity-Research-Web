Preliminary Research

Muqarnas Beyond Divinity: From Sacred Geometry to Secular Spiritual Space

Research Position

From Sacred Ornament to a Vessel for Secular Contemplation

Muqarnas is a traditional architectural form composed of layered geometric units. It decomposes continuous space into discrete honeycomb-like structures, forming a complex three-dimensional transition system. Spatially, muqarnas serves as a transition from square rooms to circular domes. Experientially, Islamic architecture creates an abstract spiritual experience through geometric order, repetitive structures, infinitely extending patterns, and the layered interplay of light, shadow, and space. Sacredness is not depicted directly, but perceived.

Muqarnas originated in the Iran-Iraq region around the tenth century, gradually became systematized during the Seljuk period, and then spread eastward and westward along the political and trade networks of the Islamic world. In its modern evolution, however, the religious significance of muqarnas has gradually declined. During the Soviet era in Uzbekistan, religious worship was systematically weakened. Today, traditional Islamic architectural elements have been de-sacralized. They are transformed into a national decorative language and integrated into modern public buildings, such as Tashkent's metro stations.

We want to create a de-religionized muqarnas: a spiritual vessel for the atheist. It is not a rejection of religious space, but a new question: when faith is no longer centered on God, can space still support spiritual experience? In this world, approximately 1.2 to 1.6 billion people have no religious affiliation. For them, spiritual experience has not disappeared, but it no longer depends on traditional symbols or rituals. Therefore, we need a new spatial language that can hold contemplation, consciousness, and a sense of being.

In Islamic architecture, muqarnas traditionally symbolizes sacred order, cosmic hierarchy, and transcendence. In this project, we do not focus on its religious meaning. Instead, we translate its inner logic: geometric repetition, layered structure, endless order, and the tension created by light moving across complex surfaces. These elements become a perceptual structure, a cognitive field shaped by repetition, layering, and light. It no longer serves theology, but becomes a framework for consciousness.

In this space, people no longer gaze upward toward the divine, but turn inward to confront and contemplate themselves. The dome is no longer a symbol of transcendence, but becomes an interface for the unfolding of consciousness. Geometry is also redefined. It is no longer a projection of sacred order, but a co-construction of reason and perception: both structural logic and sensory medium. It provides order while allowing ambiguity; it can be both calculated and felt.

This space does not define spirituality; it creates the conditions for it. It is a mihrab for everyone: no orientation, no doctrine, no fixed object of worship, only the direction of consciousness itself.
